The story

In a small building company, the week does not end on Friday evening. It ends on Sunday, in the basement office, in front of an Excel sheet.

At XP ELEC, in Thyez, that was Patrick's ritual. He started the company in 2006 with three employees; there are more than thirteen today, and the first ones are still there. His Sunday, though, did not change for years: a few hours, every week. Next week's planning to build cell by cell. Last week's hours to dig out of everyone's sheets, printed Excel tables filled in with a pencil on site and piled up on the desk, to copy out and check. The same job-site names typed ten times: for the planning, for the hours, for the accountant, for the quote you suspect you have overrun. And Monday morning starting with what was left of Sunday.

The app was born from a conversation in that office one summer, working out how much time everyone was losing. His son, who had spent every summer on the company's job sites, built it on evenings and weekends to get him out of the basement. Not to watch the hours: so that the week would close itself. On Monday morning he opens one page, approves, and heads out to the sites.

We called it Patrick because it does his job: the Sunday one, in his place. He kept Monday morning. And on Sundays, he takes an extra nap.

There is a Patrick in every company: the one who does the hours on Sunday. He is who it is for.

What it does

On site

  • The day in one tap. The job site from the planning is already filled in, and 8 hours is the default. On Monday, "Copy last week" fills the empty days.
  • No signal? It works anyway. In a cellar, entries and photos wait on the phone and go out when the network comes back.
  • The planning in your pocket. Every evening, everyone gets tomorrow's job site, with the address and the client's phone number.
  • Leave from the phone. Requested in three taps, approved in one, written into the hours straight away.
  • The photo of the panel is in the job-site folder before the van is back.
  • "I need…": the worker types what is missing, the depot prepares it, he is told when it is ready.
  • The job-site sheet in your pocket: address, contact, gate code, who is planned, even without signal.

In the office

  • Monday on one page. Weeks to approve in one click, leave to answer, who is where today.
  • Overtime counts itself, per week and per contract, with nothing to copy.
  • The month's payroll export for your accountant: one Excel file, one line per employee, absences by reason.
  • The planning as a grid, sent to the phones in one click, with a warning if you change it after sending.
  • Every job site has its folder: client, photos, plans, hours used against the quote, PDF report, and a warning when the quote runs low.
  • The time-and-materials statement, day by day, with its approval box, to staple to the invoice or e-mail to the client.
  • Rain, in one click: the site stops for bad weather, everyone is told, the accountant gets the line.
  • A link for the client, a screen for the workshop: the client follows photos and billable hours; the workshop shows who goes where, the weather and what to load.

Who it is for

Building companies with 3 to 25 employees where the boss is still out on the sites: electricians, plumbers, heating engineers, masons, carpenters, painters. The ones where the hours get done on Sunday because nobody else can do them.

Not for fifty-person firms with a design office and a payroll department: there is bigger, pricier software for them. Patrick does three things, hours, planning and leave, and does them without training.
